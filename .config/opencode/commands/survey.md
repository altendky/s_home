# Product Survey

**Research request:** $ARGUMENTS

Conduct an interactive, read-only survey of the requested product, device,
tool, component, or service category. The goal is not merely to name a winner.
Build an educational map of the market, identify genuinely comparable
solutions, explain meaningful differences, and show what remaining evidence
would narrow the choice.

Do not purchase anything, contact a vendor, create accounts, or modify files
unless the user separately and explicitly requests that action. Treat products
the user mentions as useful seeds and qualifying examples, not presumptive
winners.

## Working Principles

- Define the function before searching brands.
- Group products by mechanism and role before comparing quality or price.
- Survey manufacturers and products iteratively; each can reveal new entries
  for the other survey.
- Separate general product quality from compatibility with the user's specific
  application.
- Prefer product-specific evidence over brand reputation.
- Distinguish documented facts, corroborated claims, reasoned inferences, and
  unknowns.
- Search broadly enough to resist anchoring on the first strong candidate.
- Ask the user only questions that could materially change the research scope,
  comparison groups, or recommendation.

## Phase 1: Define The Function

Turn the request and available conversation context into a concise research
brief containing:

1. **General function**: the outcome the solution must accomplish, expressed
   without assuming a particular product style.
2. **Specific application**: the user's device, environment, workflow,
   frequency of use, and consequences of poor performance.
3. **Compatibility references**: relevant models, parts, interfaces,
   standards, diagrams, measurements, consumables, adjacent products, and
   documentation.
4. **Seed solutions**: products or mechanisms already supplied by the user or
   found during initial orientation. Explain what role each appears to fill.
5. **Preferences and constraints**: quality posture, service expectations,
   budget posture, availability, location, ergonomics, origin, ecosystem,
   longevity, and other stated priorities.
6. **Eligibility rule**: a short test every included solution must pass.
7. **Exclusion rule**: nearby product categories that look relevant but do not
   perform the required function.

Read applicable local documentation before relying on external assumptions.
When the request concerns a documented system, inspect its primary record,
active plans, diagrams, parts records, and operational context as appropriate.

If critical context is missing, ask a small set of bundled questions. If the
brief is already sufficiently specific, do not ask the user to repeat known
information.

## Phase 2: Preliminary Direction Check

Before launching a large survey:

1. Find a small, diverse set of preliminary solutions, usually two to four.
2. Confirm that each passes the eligibility rule at least provisionally.
3. Check its apparent applicability to the specific application.
4. Show the research brief, preliminary mechanism or product families, and any
   important interpretation choices to the user.
5. Ask whether the direction and scope are correct, then pause for the answer.

Skip this pause only when the user explicitly requests uninterrupted research
or has already confirmed the same brief and product direction in the current
conversation.

## Phase 3: Map The Market

Survey manufacturers deliberately across the regions and markets relevant to
the category. For globally traded physical products, default to checking North
America, Europe, Japan, and any additional region important to that category.
Explain when a region is irrelevant rather than silently omitting it.

Include different market channels where applicable:

- general-purpose manufacturers;
- industry-specific manufacturers;
- category specialists;
- professional and industrial suppliers;
- premium or tool-truck brands;
- value-oriented manufacturers when useful as market context;
- OEM, aftermarket, distributor, and private-label channels.

For each relevant manufacturer, collect enough information to understand:

- its focus on, or lack of focus on, the target category;
- its broad market placement for quality, service, value, and price;
- support and distribution model;
- manufacturing practices and product-specific country of origin when known;
- whether it designs the product, manufactures it, or appears to private-label
  another supplier's design.

Do not infer a product's country of origin from company headquarters. Similar
appearance, specifications, packaging, interfaces, or marketing language may
justify comparing products as possible relatives, but do not treat that as
proof of common design, manufacture, ownership, or private-label origin.
Describe such relationships as probable or possible unless documentary
evidence establishes them.

## Phase 4: Survey Products

Use manufacturer findings to discover products, and use product findings to
discover additional manufacturers, families, competitors, and source designs.
Continue this bidirectional loop rather than completing one survey before
starting the other.

Maintain a working ledger of serious candidates, product families, and
solution approaches discovered during research. Record enough to reconcile
each materially distinct eligible solution into the final result as a
comparison candidate, adjacent alternative, or explicit exclusion.

For each qualifying or potentially qualifying product, collect category-
appropriate evidence such as:

- exact function and operating mechanism;
- compatibility claims and exclusions;
- product family, styles, sizes, angles, capacities, and configurations;
- material, construction, accuracy, rigidity, durability, or other relevant
  performance dimensions;
- ergonomics, access, serviceability, consumables, and complementary tools;
- product-specific country of origin;
- warranty availability, scope, duration, exclusions, and service path;
- current availability and lifecycle status;
- current street price, direct price, set composition, and likely acquisition
  costs;
- credible user reports, especially reports involving the same application or
  failure mode.

Adjust the comparison dimensions to the category. For example, screwdriver
quality depends on tip geometry and handle construction, a torque wrench on
calibration and service, a router lift on rigidity and repeatability, and clip
pliers on tip geometry, alignment, and access.

Normalize price comparisons. Do not compare a single tool directly with a
multi-tool set, an unavailable listing with in-stock merchandise, or an MSRP
with a delivered street price without saying so. Include shipping, duties,
required accessories, and complementary tools when they materially affect the
choice.

## Phase 5: Evidence Discipline

Prefer sources in this order when practical:

1. Manufacturer product pages, manuals, catalogs, drawings, and support terms.
2. Authorized distributors and established specialist retailers.
3. Independent demonstrations, technical reviews, and application reports.
4. Forums and owner reports with enough detail to evaluate the application.
5. Search-result snippets and marketplace listings only as discovery leads.

Verify consequential claims at their source. Record source URLs and the date
of price or availability observations. When sources conflict, present the
conflict instead of silently choosing one.

Classify important claims internally and expose the classification when it
helps the user:

- **Documented**: stated by a primary or authoritative source.
- **Corroborated**: supported by multiple credible independent sources.
- **Inferred**: a reasoned conclusion from design, images, wording, or adjacent
  evidence.
- **Unknown**: evidence was not found or is contradictory.

Classify application compatibility separately:

- explicitly listed or tested in the exact application;
- dimensionally or technically matched;
- strongly suggested by equivalent applications;
- plausible but unverified;
- incompatible or outside the qualifying function.

Do not derive false precision from product photographs. State what an image
supports visually and what requires dimensions, a manual, or a physical test.

## Phase 6: Organize The Comparison

Build comparison groups that make products meaningfully comparable. Useful
grouping axes may include:

- operating mechanism;
- function performed in one step or multiple steps;
- access geometry, size, or capacity;
- fixed, adjustable, modular, or system-based designs;
- general-purpose versus application-specific designs;
- single products versus sets;
- service and warranty model;
- quality, value, and price position.

Start with functional and mechanical groupings. Use brand or price tiers only
after establishing that products fill the same role. Keep adjacent but
non-equivalent products visible as alternatives or complements rather than
forcing them into one ranking.

Do not collapse solutions into one family when their design differences
materially affect capability, compatibility, access, workflow, convenience,
performance, serviceability, safety, required complementary products, or total
ownership cost. Separate meaningful distinctions from superficial variations.

Within each group, explain which design differences matter in the specific
application and which are merely preferences. Call out where a premium buys an
actual feature, manufacturing control, support, or durability, and where it
appears to buy only branding or distribution.

## Phase 7: Resolve Remaining Uncertainty

List unanswered questions that could materially change the choice. For each,
recommend the least costly reliable validation method, such as:

- finding a missing manual, drawing, or product-specific origin statement;
- measuring an existing part or interface;
- checking a known compatibility reference;
- locating an exact-application user report;
- asking a manufacturer a narrowly framed technical question;
- buying from a return-friendly source and testing against a spare part;
- conducting a controlled physical fit or performance test.

Never imply that a physical test occurred. If the user supplies measurements,
photographs, or test results, incorporate them and update the comparison.

## Phase 8: Present The Educational Result

Use progressive disclosure so the user can understand the market without being
overwhelmed. Include, as appropriate:

1. The resolved function, eligibility rule, and application constraints.
2. A map of the meaningful solution mechanisms or product families.
3. Manufacturer coverage by region, specialty, and market position.
4. Comparison tables containing only genuinely comparable products.
5. Price and availability context normalized for package and acquisition.
6. Product-specific strengths, limitations, evidence quality, and compatibility
   confidence.
7. Conditional recommendations for different priorities rather than an
   unexplained universal winner.
8. Remaining questions and the recommended next validation steps.
9. Notable brands or products examined and excluded, with concise reasons when
   their absence would otherwise be surprising.

Lead with the useful conclusions, but preserve enough evidence and structure
that the user can make a different tradeoff. Clearly distinguish "best made,"
"best matched to this application," "best supported," and "best value" when
they are different products.

Before presenting the result, reconcile the working candidate ledger against
the final comparison and account for every materially distinct eligible
solution, including candidates found by parallel researchers. Keep market
coverage separate from recommendation strength: high price, weak application
evidence, or a low ranking may reduce confidence or preference, but must not
silently erase a meaningful solution family.

## Parallelism And Stopping

Use parallel research for independent regions, markets, manufacturers, or
evidence questions when the survey is substantial. Give each researcher the
same function, eligibility rule, application context, evidence requirements,
and requested output shape.

Stop broad discovery when:

- the relevant mechanisms and product families are represented;
- major category-relevant regions and market channels have been checked;
- each serious comparison group has reasonable manufacturer coverage, or its
  lack of alternatives is explained;
- additional searches are no longer changing the groupings or likely guidance;
- important unknowns have been converted into explicit validation steps.

Do not stop merely because one compelling product has been found. Do not keep
searching solely to make the candidate list longer.
